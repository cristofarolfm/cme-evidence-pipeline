# Needs assessment: therapy-induced senescence after radiotherapy and temozolomide in glioblastoma, and the case for adjuvant senolytic therapy

<!-- Drafted by Claude (Anthropic, Claude Opus 5.5 via Claude Code) from runs/senolytics_gbm/prompt_pack.md on 13 Oct 2026. Unedited before verification. -->

## Background and burden

Glioma is the most common primary brain tumor and is often associated with treatment resistance and poor prognosis [PMID:37458855].
Standard treatment typically involves radiotherapy and temozolomide-based chemotherapy, both of which induce cellular senescence [PMID:37458855].
Senescent cells remain viable and secrete a large number of factors, known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), that paradoxically have pro-tumorigenic effects [PMID:37458855].
Glioblastoma due to recurrence is clinically challenging, with 10-15 months overall survival [PMID:39021199].
Radiation also harms the normal brain: whole brain irradiation causes progressive cognitive dysfunction in ~50% of surviving patients [PMID:31960269].

## Current evidence

**Senescent tumor cells.** Irradiated GBM cell lines and patient-derived xenograft cultures senesce rapidly in a p21-dependent manner and secrete SASP factors, prominently IL-6, that activate JAK-STAT3 and NF-κB signaling in non-senescent GBM cells [PMID:39972068].
SMAC mimetics targeting cIAP2 act as senolytics in senescent GBM cells, and the SMAC mimetic birinapant given as an adjuvant after radiotherapy prevented the emergence of recurrent tumors in PDX and immunocompetent mouse models [PMID:39972068].
Temozolomide-induced therapy-induced senescence in glioma cells is determined by the p21CIP1-CDK1/2 axis and does not require activation of the DREAM complex [PMID:40319068].
Residual senescent GBM cells activate a PERK-mediated unfolded protein response, and PERK inhibition acted as a senolytic and prevented recurrence in vitro and in vivo [PMID:39021199].
Chemotherapy-induced senescent glioblastoma cells had a tumor-promoting function and reduced survival in male mice through DDX58-mediated STAT1 signaling [PMID:40259469].

**Senescent cells in the irradiated brain.** After cranial irradiation, widespread senescence occurred in the mouse brain, with astrocytes being particularly susceptible, and senescent astrocytes secreted HGF to activate Met in glioma cells [PMID:34580063].
The senolytic drug ABT-263 (navitoclax) selectively killed senescent astrocytes in vivo and significantly attenuated growth of glioma cells implanted in preirradiated brains [PMID:34580063].
Senescent astrocytes also promoted an immunosuppressive microenvironment by enhancing recruitment of myeloid inflammatory cells, and senolytic agents given after radiotherapy substantially prolonged survival in preclinical models [PMID:38342629].
IL6 was the predominant SASP factor elevated in irradiated astrocytes, and ABT-263 or tocilizumab abrogated JAK2/STAT3 activation in GBM cells [PMID:41385178].
All-trans retinoic acid eliminated senescent astrocytes, improved survival in GBM-bearing mice and alleviated radiation-induced cognitive impairment [PMID:39513361].

**Protecting normal brain function.** In mice given fractionated whole brain irradiation (5 Gy twice weekly for 4 weeks), depleting senescent cells with ABT263/navitoclax at 3 months rescued neurovascular coupling and improved cognitive performance [PMID:31960269].
In the same irradiation model, navitoclax eliminated senescent endothelial cells and decreased blood-brain barrier permeability [PMID:37642933].
Radiation-induced pericyte senescence contributed to blood-brain barrier dysfunction and glioma growth, and senolytic drugs significantly mitigated radiation-induced cognitive dysfunction [PMID:39110121].

**Other tumor types and agents.** In H3K27M-altered diffuse midline glioma models, irradiation induced senescence and navitoclax acted as a senolytic primarily via Bcl-xL inhibition [PMID:41430412].
Senescent pilocytic astrocytoma cells upregulate BCL-XL and depend on it for survival [PMID:35977048].
Temozolomide-induced senescent U87MG cells became sensitive to DR5-B-mediated apoptosis, but p53-mutated, MGMT-proficient T98G cells did not [PMID:41249587].

**Clinical landscape.** The only registered study identified is an early phase 1 trial combining dasatinib, quercetin and fisetin with temozolomide, LMP744 and an autologous vaccine in previously treated glioma with residual disease, with an enrollment of 30 [NCT:NCT07025226].

## Practice gaps

Clinicians who manage glioblastoma after chemoradiation should recognize that radiotherapy and temozolomide leave behind viable senescent cells that can drive recurrence, yet these cells are not currently measured or targeted in standard care [PMID:37458855, PMID:38030881].
Senolytic agents are being discussed for latent glioma, but the evidence remains preclinical, and clinicians need to distinguish this experimental rationale from established practice [PMID:38030881, NCT:NCT07025226].
Cognitive decline after brain irradiation is common, and clinicians should be aware that preclinical data link it to senescent astrocytes, endothelial cells and pericytes [PMID:31960269, PMID:37642933, PMID:39110121].

## Evidence gaps

Timing is unresolved: in irradiated INK-ATTAC mice, early clearance of p16-positive cells at 1 month had no effect, whereas later clearance at 4 months reduced frailty, restored blood-brain barrier integrity and increased median survival [PMID:42649478].
Responses differ by tumor genotype, as senescence features and senolytic sensitization were seen in p53-proficient, MGMT-deficient cells but not in p53-mutated, MGMT-proficient cells [PMID:41249587].
Which senescent compartment matters most, tumor cells or non-neoplastic astrocytes, endothelial cells and pericytes, has not been compared directly [PMID:39972068, PMID:34580063, PMID:39110121].
No efficacy data in patients exist; the only registered trial measures feasibility outcomes such as completion of 3 cycles [NCT:NCT07025226].

3. Identify the open questions (timing, biomarkers, tumor genotype) that clinical trials of senolytics in glioma must answer.
